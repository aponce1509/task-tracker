Based on [^1]. I applied the concepts learned creating its note.
[[Capture Criteria How to Avoid Keeping Too Much (or Too Little)]].

## The PDF Phase

This [^2] is not unique of pdf documents. It's the phase where you are reading the
document while taking note. YOU NEED TO START TAKING NOTES TO MAKE THIS WORK. If
you realise that the content is interesting at the end you will lose it.

In this phase, you just will annotate the document or take notes on a paper or a
blank markdown file.

That mostly all. Think of you have a book where you have notes and post-its.
When you need to check something you go to it and revisit it.

The only improve I could suggest is making a list of references for each PAR you
are working on. This allows you know which content you have seen and if you need
to solve a question can use this curated info that will be faster.

## How to capture in the Second Brain

However there is content that is note worthy, because of its relevance for the
project, it is recurrently asked, or it is super important for me.

This process should be quick but manual. The overhead is:

- Add tags
- Copy the content
- Adding some personal point of view

## The Reference List

<!-- NOTE: #output/second_brain Reference List: a Second Brain element -->

It is just a markdown file where you write down references that you are using
for a PAR. You can give context to the references and if there is annotation
indicate where it is:

- PDF
- Zotero
- Epub
- Reader
- A link to a note

Even you can make sub-list if you have made a capture and want to keep track of
it here.

## Extractions that inspired this document

Extracted from [^1]

Knowledge isn't just wise quotations from long-dead Greek philosophers in white
togas. It’s not just the teachings found within thick textbooks written by
academics with advanced degrees. In the digital world we live in, knowledge most
often shows up as “content”—snippets of text, screenshots, bookmarked articles,
podcasts, or other kinds of media. This includes the content you gather from
outside sources but also the content you create as you compose emails, draw up
project plans, brainstorm ideas, and journal your own thoughts.

Knowledge assets can come from either the external world or your inner thoughts.
External knowledge could include:

- Highlights: Insightful passages from books or articles you read.
- Quotes: Memorable passages from podcasts or audiobooks you listen to.
- Bookmarks and favorites: Links to interesting content you find on the web or
  favorited social media posts.
- Voice memos: Clips recorded on your mobile device as “notes to self.”
- Meeting notes: Notes you take about what was discussed during meetings or
  phone calls.
- Images: Photos or other images that you find inspiring or interesting.
- Takeaways: Lessons from courses, conferences, or presentations you’ve
  attended.
